Why worked examples matter more than raw formulas

A formula on its own is easy to misread. It's easy to plug a number into the wrong slot, mix up centimetres and inches, or apply a multiplier meant for a different equation entirely. A worked example removes that ambiguity — you can hold your own numbers up against the sample calculation, line by line, and see exactly where you'd diverge.

This matters even more in health and fitness, where a small input error can shift a result meaningfully: a calorie target that's off by a few hundred calories, or a macro split that's quietly unbalanced. Worked examples act as a built-in check, letting you verify your own result rather than simply trusting a number that appeared on a screen.

BMI calculator — a worked example

Body mass index estimates whether a person's weight falls within a typical range for their height. It's a screening tool, not a diagnosis, but it remains one of the most searched health calculations because it's quick and requires only two inputs.

Sample person
  • Height: 170 cm (1.70 m)
  • Weight: 68 kg
  • Formula: weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²)
Calculation: 68 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) = 68 ÷ 2.89 = 23.5

A BMI of 23.5 sits within the commonly cited "typical weight" band of 18.5–24.9. Because the formula only accounts for height and weight, it doesn't distinguish muscle from fat — which is why it's usually read alongside other measurements rather than on its own.

Calorie & TDEE calculator — a worked example

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) estimates how many calories a person burns in a day, combining their resting metabolic rate with activity level. It's the starting point for almost any calorie target, whether the goal is maintenance, a deficit, or a surplus.

Sample person
  • Age: 30 · Sex: female · Height: 165 cm · Weight: 62 kg
  • Resting rate (Mifflin-St Jeor): (10 × 62) + (6.25 × 165) − (5 × 30) − 161
  • Activity level: moderately active, multiplier ×1.55
Resting rate: 620 + 1031.25 − 150 − 161 = 1340.25 kcal · TDEE: 1340.25 × 1.55 ≈ 2,077 kcal/day

To lose roughly 0.5 kg a week, this person would aim for a daily deficit of around 500 calories, landing near 1,577 kcal a day — a target derived entirely from the two lines of arithmetic above, not an arbitrary guess.

Macronutrient calculator — a worked example

Once a calorie target is set, macros divide it into protein, carbohydrate, and fat. A common split for general fitness is 30% protein, 40% carbohydrate, and 30% fat, though the ratio shifts depending on the goal.

Sample person
  • Daily target: 2,000 kcal
  • Split: 30% protein · 40% carbohydrate · 30% fat
  • Protein & carbs: 4 kcal/gram · Fat: 9 kcal/gram
Protein: 600 kcal ÷ 4 = 150 g · Carbs: 800 kcal ÷ 4 = 200 g · Fat: 600 kcal ÷ 9 ≈ 67 g

Those three figures — 150 g protein, 200 g carbohydrate, 67 g fat — are what a food-tracking app would ask you to hit, and each one traces back directly to the calorie percentage it was built from.

Body fat percentage calculator — a worked example

The U.S. Navy method estimates body fat using a handful of tape-measure circumferences instead of specialised equipment, which is why it's one of the more accessible estimation methods.

Sample person (male)
  • Waist: 85 cm · Neck: 38 cm · Height: 178 cm
  • Formula: 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077 × log10(waist − neck) + 0.15456 × log10(height)) − 450
Working through the logarithms gives an estimated body fat of roughly 15–16%

Tape-based estimates like this one carry a margin of error of a few percentage points, so they're best used to track a trend over weeks and months rather than treated as a precise, one-off reading.

One-rep max calculator — a worked example

One-rep max (1RM) estimates the heaviest weight a person could lift for a single repetition, calculated from a lighter set taken to near-failure — useful for programming training percentages without needing to test a true max every session.

Sample lift
  • Weight lifted: 80 kg for 6 reps
  • Epley formula: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)
80 × (1 + 6 ÷ 30) = 80 × 1.2 = 96 kg estimated 1RM

From that 96 kg figure, a training percentage of 75% would call for roughly 72 kg — the kind of number a program builder uses to set weight across an entire training block.

How to choose the right calculator for your goal

Start with the outcome you're trying to understand, not the calculator that sounds most familiar. If you want a general health check-in, BMI and body fat are the natural pair. If you're adjusting your diet, TDEE and macros work together — the first sets the total, the second divides it. If you're training for strength, one-rep max feeds directly into how much weight you load on the bar each week.

Common mistakes when using health calculators

The most frequent error is unit confusion — entering pounds where kilograms are expected, or inches where centimetres are expected. The second is treating a single estimate as fixed rather than as a starting point that gets adjusted after two or three weeks of real-world results. The third is skipping the activity-level honesty check on TDEE calculators, which throws off every number calculated afterward.
